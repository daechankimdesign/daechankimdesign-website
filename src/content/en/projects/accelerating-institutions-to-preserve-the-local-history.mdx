## The Problem

Oral histories are still collected much as they always have been. Staff recruit participants, schedule interviews, sit for each recording, and then transcribe and analyze it all by hand. Every step costs staff hours, and libraries in particular are short of staff. The stories a community most wants to keep are the ones there is never enough time to gather.

One campaign on Oria today comes from the College Park Aviation Museum. Called Reclaiming Our Space, it asks neighbors what Lakeland means to them, with topics titled "Care, belong, protect, preserve" and "Safe Spaces."

The need was sharpest in senior living, assisted living, local libraries, and cultural initiatives. All of them hold stories that cannot be replaced, and none had a realistic way to collect them or a tool built for the institution's side.

## Talking to the Stakeholders

We were bootstrapping, so the product was shaped by conversations with the people who would use it. To move from a rough alpha to something real, I interviewed two librarians, a historic-resources lead from Maryland's park and planning commission, and a therapist who works with personal narrative. Their guidance kept steering the build from then on.

Two findings reset the plan. First, they welcomed AI, and they cared deeply about ownership. The collection had to belong to the institution rather than to Oria. Second, and more surprising, these institutions already have trusted channels to their participants.

**What ate their time was collecting and analyzing the stories.** They did not need help with distribution or recruitment the way a consumer product would, so I aimed the product at collection and analysis instead. A campaign had to be quick to create and share, and collected stories easy to manage and understand. Telling a story had to feel worth the effort, so that people would share a real one.

## The Platform

An organization signs up, creates its space, and opens a campaign, which sets the overall theme of a collection. Under it, the organization adds topics, each with its own questions. To save organizers from a blank page, the platform can draft the questions with the Gemini API from the campaign, the topic, and the direction the organizer enters. The drafts appear where organizers write and edit the questionnaire, and they can be edited at any time, like any question an organizer wrote. Each campaign and topic publishes to missionoria.com, ready to share as a link or QR code, and participants can also browse public campaigns to find a local institution collecting stories near them.

The collection belongs to the institution, which can download every story in it. Each teller keeps ownership of their own story and chooses whether it is public, community-only, or private, or submits it anonymously. Choosing public or community-only grants Oria a license to store, display, and share the story for historical, educational, and research purposes.

The platform also gives organizations analytics their old process could not, including participation, completion, and retention rates and the number of stories collected, so a small team can see what is working without counting by hand.

## Designing for Two Users at Once

The hard part was that this is two products sharing one platform. The institution needs control, speed, and clarity, so that creating, deploying, and managing a collection takes as little effort as possible. The participant needs a calm space that makes telling a real story feel worth doing, which is the opposite of an admin tool. The central design challenge was holding both in one system without letting the operational side make the teller's side feel cold.

Each need became a specific decision in the screens. A teller joins with an organization lookup or a join code, with no account to create. Each screen asks a single question, with read-aloud and microphone icons beside the prompt, so a question can be heard and answered by voice. An answer can be typed, recorded as audio or video, or uploaded, and a story can go in anonymously. On the institution side, a campaign is one link and one QR code, and every story arrives transcribed and ready to review.

I designed in Figma and built mostly with Claude Code and Codex, on React and AWS after an initial Vercel build. The data engineer structured the database and set up the build and deploy pipelines.

## Learnings

Moving fast cost consistency. The project went through many design phases and a shifting set of tools in a few months. The technology let me build quickly, but it also let the work sprawl, and the visual design has not caught up yet. I am still going screen by screen to bring everything back to one design system. That is the cost of building this fast with AI tools. The work can get ahead of its own conventions, and then it has to go back and fix them.
