I started where people discuss money openly, in online communities. Coding three threads by what people say, want, and value, four of the eight insights pointed at one difficulty: knowing which financial decision is the efficient one. Four interviews with young adults at four career stages, in South Korea, California, Massachusetts, and Maryland, traced that difficulty to missing information, personalized support, and confidence, and showed each stage needing a different kind of support.

<Dek>"I wish I just had a financial advisor that actually had my best interest in heart." Interviewee 02</Dek>

That sentence reframed the opportunity. The missing thing was not information, which is public and abundant. It was that nothing was watching this particular account and saying, at the moment it mattered, what a given choice would cost.

## The Approach

The core idea is proactive support rather than answers on request. The assistant monitors the account continuously, flags issues as they emerge, and supplies the information needed to work through them, while authorship over the money stays with the person.

The features were drawn from the three gaps the research confirmed. Confidence calls for visible progress: a financial health index, small changes shown over time, badges and streaks. Information calls for plain language, comprehensive reviews of consequences, and source-linked answers. Personalized calls for an analyzed profile and support that follows it.

Keeping the flow conventional mattered as much as the intelligence behind it. Anyone already used to a banking app should be able to onboard and start exploring without relearning anything, so the unfamiliar part is the system's behavior, not the navigation.

## The Journey

The journey runs in four parts, and each has one job. Setup comes first, so nobody arrives at an empty application, and the service can be explored before any commitment. Payment is where the idea has to hold up. Profile comes last, because improvements to financial health are otherwise too small to notice month to month.

## The Product

**Home opens on a forecast, not a balance.** The first thing on the screen is what the month is heading toward, set against the two months before it, so the number arrives while there is still time to act on it. The alert sits directly under it. Below that come the payment streak, the forecasted credit score, and the categorized budget the assistant proposed during onboarding.

**The card detail page makes the monitoring concrete.** It holds the full record of how the card is being used, from individual transactions to the utilization rate over time. Every entry is categorized automatically, so the picture of where the money goes requires no tagging, and a category running ahead of its usual pace is named. The projection is the point of the page: the assistant reads the pace so far, calculates the end-of-month balance, and raises it while the month is still open rather than reporting on it once the statement closes.

**The payment screen prices each option.** The amount is set with a slider that stops at four fixed points: the minimum payment, the assistant's suggestion, the full payment, and the current statement balance. Each stop changes the message beneath it, so the trade-off appears at the moment of the decision instead of on a later statement.

At the minimum, the screen states what paying only that amount will cost in interest. Directly beneath, the assistant's advice prices the alternative, a fixed payment, and what it saves. Both messages are tappable, and both lead to the reasoning behind them.

The minimum payment path is deliberately the most supported one, since that is where the original interview insight came from. It would have been easier to discourage the minimum and move on. Explaining it is the harder and more useful thing.

**A warning opens its reasoning, with sources.** Tapping the warning opens Zeto AI on the mechanism behind it: what a minimum payment is, and why paying only that amount keeps the account in good standing while the debt keeps growing. The explanation is drawn from existing documentation and lists its sources, so the reasoning can be checked rather than taken on trust.

**The follow-up is asked in place, and answered with arithmetic.** The natural next question is the one the interviews surfaced: what happens if I only pay the minimum? The answer, drawn from three source documents, works it through: how many months the balance takes to clear and what it costs in interest, set against a fixed monthly payment. It ends in a button that updates the payment, so the decision is made without leaving the flow. The suggestion stays a suggestion: the user still sets the amount.

## Learnings

**One sentence carried the whole project.** A participant who did not understand what a minimum payment meant did more to shape the design than any of the synthesis that surrounded it. Making the minimum payment path the most supported one, rather than the most discouraged one, followed directly from that sentence.

**Proactive and pushy are separated by a single design decision.** A system that monitors an account and raises problems unprompted is one step away from one that nags. What keeps it on the right side is that every intervention states a number and then stops. The assistant proposes an amount and shows its working, and the user still sets the amount.

**What is still untested.** This is a concept, and it was never put in front of the people it was researched with. The open question is whether a consequence priced in dollars changes behavior, or whether it becomes one more number to dismiss. Several things the design depends on were not designed or tested either: what the assistant says when its forecast turns out wrong, how often an alert can fire before it becomes noise, what consent connecting a card asks for, and whether the slider's Full and Statement labels mean anything to someone who did not know what a minimum payment does. Only testing would settle them.
